## Passage 1

When construction began on the Erie Canal in 1817, critics called it a ditch that would drain the state treasury and reach nowhere worth going. The plan was audacious: a waterway 363 miles long, cut through forest and rock, connecting the Hudson River at Albany to Lake Erie at Buffalo. New York had no trained engineers at the time. The men who surveyed and supervised the work were largely self-taught lawyers and judges who learned the profession as they built it.

The obstacles were considerable. Near Lockport the route met a limestone escarpment rising sixty feet. Workers cut through it with black powder and hand tools and built a double flight of five locks to carry boats up the face. Farther east, an aqueduct of stone arches carried the canal over the Genesee River. Laborers, many of them recent immigrants, worked for roughly eighty cents a day and were frequently sickened by malaria contracted in the swamps west of Syracuse.

The canal opened in October 1825. Its effect on commerce was immediate. Before the canal, shipping a ton of freight from Buffalo to New York City cost about one hundred dollars and took three weeks. Afterward it cost roughly ten dollars and took eight days. Farmers in Ohio and Indiana who had been unable to sell grain beyond their own counties suddenly had access to Atlantic markets. Towns along the route — Rochester, Syracuse, Utica — grew from villages into cities within a decade.

The financial return was equally striking. Tolls repaid the canal's entire seven-million-dollar construction cost within nine years. New York City, which had been the fourth-largest port in the country, became the first, and it has never surrendered that position.

Perhaps the canal's most lasting legacy was less tangible. The project trained a generation of American engineers who went on to build the nation's railroads. Men who learned to move earth and water on the Erie carried that knowledge west, and the methods they developed on a ditch in upstate New York shaped construction practice for the rest of the century.

## Passage 2

A honeybee colony maintains the temperature of its brood nest within a narrow range, usually between 93 and 95 degrees Fahrenheit, regardless of conditions outside the hive. Developing larvae are sensitive to variation. Bees raised even a few degrees too cool emerge with shortened lifespans and impaired ability to learn, while overheating is fatal outright.

In cold weather the colony forms a cluster. Bees pack together around the brood, and those in the interior disengage their wings from the flight muscles and vibrate those muscles rapidly. The motion produces heat without producing flight. Bees on the outer shell of the cluster sit still, their bodies acting as insulation, and they periodically rotate inward to warm themselves while others take their place at the surface. A cluster can hold its core near ninety degrees while the air outside sits below zero.

Summer presents the opposite problem. Foragers collect water rather than nectar and spread it in thin films across the comb. Other workers fan their wings to move air through the hive, and the evaporating water carries heat away. The arrangement is essentially an evaporative cooler, efficient enough that a hive in full sun on a ninety-five degree day may be cooler inside than out.

What makes the system notable is that no individual bee perceives the hive's overall temperature or directs the response. Each bee reacts to conditions in its immediate vicinity according to a threshold that varies slightly from bee to bee. Some begin fanning at a lower temperature than others. Because those thresholds are distributed across the population, the colony's response is graduated rather than all-or-nothing: a small rise in temperature recruits a few fanners, a larger rise recruits many. The regulation emerges from the aggregate of thousands of independent local decisions, with no coordination from above.

## Passage 3

Every year workers are injured by machinery they believed was shut off. The hazard is not usually a failure of equipment but a failure of assumption: someone restores power to a circuit while another person is still working on it. The procedure developed to prevent this is called lockout/tagout, and its logic is simple enough that its effectiveness depends less on technical sophistication than on discipline.

The procedure begins with identifying every energy source feeding the equipment. This is less obvious than it sounds. A machine may draw electrical power from more than one panel, and it may also store energy in ways that are not visible: compressed air in a reservoir, hydraulic fluid under pressure, a raised component held up only by gravity, a capacitor that retains charge after disconnection. Each of these must be isolated, released, or physically blocked.

Once the sources are isolated, each worker involved applies a personal lock to the disconnect. The locks are keyed individually, and no worker holds a key to anyone else's. Consequently the equipment cannot be re-energized until every person who applied a lock has returned and removed it. A tag accompanies each lock, naming the worker and the date, but the tag alone is not sufficient. A tag is a warning; a lock is a physical barrier.

The final step is verification. Before beginning work, the worker attempts to start the equipment using its normal controls and confirms with test instruments that no voltage is present. This step is frequently skipped because it feels redundant, and it is precisely the step that catches the mislabeled panel or the second feed nobody knew about.

Most serious incidents trace back to a shortcut taken by an experienced worker rather than a mistake made by a new one. Familiarity breeds a confidence that the procedure is designed to make unnecessary.

## Passage 4

Before 1883 there was no standard time in the United States. Each town set its clocks by local noon, the moment the sun stood highest overhead. Because noon arrives progressively later as one travels west, a clock in Boston read several minutes ahead of one in New York, which in turn ran ahead of Philadelphia. For most purposes this hardly mattered. A farmer had no need to know the hour in a city three hundred miles away.

Railroads changed the calculation. A train traveling between cities passed through dozens of local times, and a schedule written in one town was unreliable in the next. Railroad companies responded by adopting their own standard times, usually keyed to the clock at a major terminal, but each company chose independently. Pittsburgh's station displayed six different clocks. A traveler changing trains had to know not only the departure hour but which railroad's version of that hour applied. Missed connections were routine, and collisions on shared track were a genuine danger when two crews disagreed about the time.

On November 18, 1883, the railroads acted jointly. At noon, telegraph signals transmitted a single standard time to stations across the country, and the continent was divided into four zones, each an hour apart. Newspapers called it the Day of Two Noons, since in many cities clocks were set back and the hour of noon occurred twice.

The change was not universally welcomed. Some communities objected that a distant corporation had no authority to say what time it was, and a number of towns kept local time in defiance for years. Cincinnati held out until 1890. But the practical advantage was overwhelming, and adoption spread on its own.

Notably, the federal government played no role. Congress did not ratify standard time zones until 1918, thirty-five years after the railroads had established them and long after the public had accepted them as ordinary.

## Passage 5

Malcolm McLean was a trucking executive, not a shipowner, and the problem that interested him was not sailing but waiting. In the 1930s he spent a day parked at a North Carolina dock watching longshoremen unload his trailer piece by piece and carry the pieces onto a ship. The cargo itself moved perhaps a hundred feet. It took most of the day.

Breakbulk loading, as the method was called, dominated shipping into the 1950s. Goods arrived at a port in barrels, crates, and sacks of varying size. Gangs of longshoremen sorted them, stacked them on pallets, hoisted them by crane into the hold, and stowed them by hand so the load would not shift at sea. A midsize freighter might spend more days in port being loaded than it spent crossing the ocean. Labor accounted for most of the total cost of moving goods by sea, and theft from open cargo was so routine that companies budgeted for it.

McLean's insight was that a ship should carry the truck trailer's box rather than its contents. In 1956 he loaded fifty-eight containers onto a converted tanker and sailed from Newark to Houston. The cost of loading fell from roughly $5.86 per ton to about sixteen cents.

Adoption was slower than those numbers suggest. Containers are useful only if the cranes, trucks, railcars, and ports at both ends can handle them, which meant the industry had to agree on dimensions before anyone could invest confidently. Standardization took another decade of negotiation. Ports that committed early, such as Oakland and Rotterdam, grew enormously. Older ports that could not accommodate the cranes, including much of the New York waterfront, declined and never recovered.

The consequences reached well beyond shipping. When transport costs approach insignificance, it becomes rational to manufacture a component wherever it is cheapest and assemble it somewhere else entirely. The container did not cause globalization, but it is difficult to imagine the scale of modern supply chains without it.

## Passage 6

Steel expands when heated and contracts when cooled, and the amount is predictable. On a small object the change is invisible. On a bridge it is not. A steel span a thousand feet long will grow about eight inches between a January morning and a July afternoon, and a structure that cannot accommodate that movement will relieve the stress on its own — by buckling, by cracking its deck, or by shearing the bolts that hold it to its supports.

The standard solution is the expansion joint, a deliberate gap in the roadway bridged by interlocking steel fingers or a flexible seal. The gap is widest in cold weather and nearly closed in heat. Drivers experience it as the rhythmic thump at the ends of a span. Engineers size these joints for the temperature range the structure will actually see, which is why bridges in Minnesota carry larger joints than bridges in Florida.

Bearings serve a related purpose at the supports. Rather than fixing both ends of a span rigidly, designers typically fix one end and allow the other to slide on steel rollers or a low-friction polymer pad. The span is thus anchored against wind and traffic loads while remaining free to lengthen and shorten.

These details are among the first to fail from neglect. Expansion joints collect road salt, grit, and water. Seals tear. Bearings seize with corrosion. A frozen bearing does not announce itself. The span continues to expand regardless, and the force it generates is enormous, transferring into piers and abutments never designed to resist it. Inspectors consequently pay disproportionate attention to components that carry no load at all, because the failure of a joint is rarely confined to the joint.

## Passage 1

Fog is simply a cloud that forms at ground level, and it appears whenever air is cooled to the point where it can no longer hold its moisture as vapor. Radiation fog is the most common type inland. On a clear, calm night the ground radiates away the heat it absorbed during the day, chilling the air directly above it. Because clouds would trap that heat, radiation fog requires a clear sky, and because wind would mix the chilled layer with warmer air above, it requires nearly still conditions. It typically forms before dawn and burns off within a few hours of sunrise. Advection fog is different. It occurs when warm, moist air moves horizontally over a cold surface, such as ocean water or late-season snow. Because it is driven by moving air rather than still air, advection fog can persist through the day and can form in steady wind.

A ladder placed at the wrong angle is the most common cause of ladder falls. The working rule is four to one: the base should sit one foot away from the wall for every four feet of height to the point of support. The ladder should be secured at the top, and the ground beneath it must be level and firm. Workers should face the ladder while climbing and maintain three points of contact — two hands and a foot, or two feet and a hand. ## Passage 3

Beavers are often treated as pests, but their dams produce effects that engineers spend considerable money to reproduce. A beaver dam slows moving water, and slowed water drops the silt it carries. Over time the sediment builds up behind the dam and creates a wetland. That wetland absorbs runoff during heavy rain and releases it slowly afterward, which reduces flooding downstream and keeps streams flowing later into a dry summer. The standing water also raises the local water table, benefiting vegetation well beyond the pond itself. The wetlands support fish, amphibians, and birds that cannot live in a fast, narrow channel. Beavers were nearly eliminated from much of North America by the fur trade, and streams that lost them became straighter, faster, and more prone to both flooding and drying. Some watershed managers now reintroduce beavers deliberately, or build low structures that imitate their dams, as a cheaper alternative to conventional flood control.

Before the telegraph, information moved no faster than a person could carry it. A message from New York to New Orleans took weeks. Samuel Morse demonstrated a working electric telegraph in 1844, and within twenty years wire connected most American cities. Newspapers, which had reprinted news days or weeks old, began publishing events reported the same day. The telegraph also created the first occupation in which women were widely employed outside the home in technical work.

## Passage 6

The apprenticeship model is older than the industrial economy that now relies on it. Medieval guilds bound a young person to a master for a set term, during which the apprentice received instruction, room, and board but little or no wage. The arrangement protected the trade's standards and controlled entry into it. Modern apprenticeship retains the core structure — supervised work paired with classroom instruction — but inverts the economics. An apprentice today is paid from the first day and earns scheduled increases as hours accumulate, so the skill is acquired without debt. The classroom portion is no longer informal. Registered programs specify a minimum number of instructional hours per year alongside a required number of on-the-job hours, and completion produces a credential recognized across state lines. What has not changed is the central premise: that certain work is learned by doing it under supervision, and that no amount of classroom time alone produces competence in it.

## Passage 7

Sound travels through a wall in two ways, and the distinction matters because the remedies differ. Airborne sound — voices, music, traffic — strikes the wall surface and vibrates it, and the vibrating wall radiates the sound into the next room. Mass resists this. A heavy wall vibrates less than a light one, which is why a masonry wall blocks conversation better than a stud wall of the same thickness. Structure-borne sound is different. Footsteps, a slammed door, or a pump bolted to a floor inject vibration directly into the building frame, and that vibration travels through the structure and re-radiates far from its source. Adding mass does little against it. The effective remedy is to interrupt the path with a resilient connection: a rubber isolator under the equipment, a floating floor, or resilient channel that separates the drywall from the studs. A common and expensive mistake is to treat a structure-borne problem with more insulation, which addresses the wrong mechanism and produces almost no improvement.

## Where People Lose Points

**Answering from knowledge instead of the passage.** Several wrong choices here are true statements about the real world that the passage never makes. On the safety and technical passages especially, if you know the trade, your knowledge will actively work against you. The only question is what the text says.

**NOT / EXCEPT questions.** Question 15 flips the task. Under time pressure people find a listed item and select it. Slow down for one extra second whenever you see a negative in the stem.

**"Best" answers versus "acceptable" answers.** Question 30 is the model: choice A is close enough that a rushed reader takes it, but the passage explicitly rules it out. When two answers both look right, the more precisely qualified one is almost always correct.

**Running out of time on Part B.** Thirty-five seconds per question means roughly 90 seconds to read a short passage and 25 seconds per question after that. Read once, answer immediately, don't go back.

## What to Do Next

1. **Take Part B under a hard 25-minute timer before you take Part A.** It will tell you more about your weak points than the untimed version ever could.
2. **Read something dense for 20 minutes a day** — a technical manual, a long-form news article, a safety standard. Not fiction. The skill being tested is sustained attention to unfamiliar factual prose.
3. **For every question you miss, find the sentence in the passage that proves the right answer.** If you can't find it, the question was testing inference and you should reread how the correct answer was built.
4. **Combine both files into one full-length sitting once you're comfortable** — math section, short break, reading section. That's the real test-day experience, and stamina is part of it.
